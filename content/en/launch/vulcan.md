Vulcan is ASTRO's in-house hybrid rocket engine, and the core of what makes us
unique: we are the only student organisation in Norway developing a hybrid
engine. A hybrid burns a solid fuel grain with a liquid oxidiser — it can be shut
down mid-burn, and is safer to handle than a pure solid motor.

Instead of a launch, we ran a **static fire**: the engine is clamped into a test
stand and fired on the ground, so we can measure thrust, combustion and behaviour
under controlled conditions — without sending anything into the air. This is how
serious engine development is done, one step at a time.

The test is an important step towards flying on our own engine rather than
depending on imports — a lesson we took from [Altair](/en/launch/altair).

## Key figures

- **Thrust:** roughly 700–750 N steady, with a peak of about 1.7 kN at ignition
- **Burn time:** about 4 seconds
- **Fuel:** paraffin wax
- **Oxidiser:** nitrous oxide (N₂O)
- **Chamber pressure:** designed for 30 bar

## The engine

![Machined Vulcan parts before assembly: nozzle, pre-chamber, composite chamber liners and the finished engine](/images/launches/Vulcan/vulcan-deler.png)

Vulcan is built from scratch: nozzle, injector, chamber liners and pressure
structure are all sized, machined and assembled by members. The combustion
chamber is lined to survive the temperature for the full burn.

![Servo-actuated ball valve mounted in a bracket, with the servo below the valve body](/images/launches/Vulcan/vulcan-ventil.png)

The oxidiser is fed through a servo-actuated ball valve. That is what gives the
hybrid its big advantage: we can shut the engine down whenever we want, both in a
planned shutdown and if something looks wrong during the test.

![CFD simulation of the flow through the injector, colour-scaled for velocity up to 62 m/s](/images/launches/Vulcan/vulcan-cfd.png)

The injector was simulated in CFD before it was machined, to see how the nitrous
distributes into the chamber.

## The test

![Vulcan mounted horizontally on the test stand in the field, with a thrust sensor behind the engine](/images/launches/Vulcan/vulcan-testrigg.jpeg)

The stand holds the engine horizontally against a load cell, so thrust is logged
through the whole burn. Filling and ignition are done from a distance.

![Expected thrust curve from the calculations: a fast rise to about 1230 N, tailing off slowly across five seconds](/images/launches/Vulcan/vulcan-thrust-forventet.png)

This is what we expected the curve to look like — calculated at a specific
impulse of 230 seconds.

![Measured thrust curve from the test: a peak of about 1690 N at ignition, then a steady plateau around 700–750 N for just over four seconds before dropping to zero](/images/launches/Vulcan/vulcan-thrust-malt.png)

And this is what we got. The engine lit, built pressure and held a steady plateau
for the whole burn before shutting down cleanly. The level sits below the
predicted one, with a sharp spike at ignition — exactly the kind of discrepancy a
static fire exists to reveal, and what we carry into the next iteration.

## Status

Completed. The engine ran the full burn, and we have real data to build on — one
step closer to a launch that is entirely our own.
